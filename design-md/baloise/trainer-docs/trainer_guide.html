Trainer Guide

Prompting Essentials and Advanced Prompting — how to run the two sessions.

You are delivering one of the most visible sessions of the AI Week. Around 1,730 colleagues have registered and we have more than 5,500 session sign-ups. The AI Week runs in this form for the first time, and prompting is the topic most people signed up for.

The slides tell you what to say. This guide tells you how to run it: how the sessions are built, where the time goes, what to prepare, and what a session looks like when it worked. Read the section for your session; the delivery principles apply to both.

The one thing to internalise Both sessions teach a method, not a set of prompts. Participants should leave able to repeat a sequence on their own work — not holding a copy of your examples.

2 Session objectives and learning objectives

Session 1 — Prompting Essentials

Objective. Turn one task from your own work into a prompt that briefs AI properly — and improve it when the first result is not good enough.

The sentence a participant should be able to say afterwards (it is on slide 2, and it is worth reading out):

“I can turn a work task into a good prompt and improve it.”

Learning objectives. By the end of the session, participants can:

  • write a prompt that states the task, the context and the format;
  • recognise what the model cannot know unless they supply it;
  • check a result rather than trust it — figures, names, dates, audience fit;
  • improve a result by changing one instruction, not by rewriting everything;
  • apply the data rules: describe the situation, never paste the data.

Session 2 — Advanced Prompting

Objective. Use advanced prompting techniques to get more consistent, controllable and reusable results from AI.

Learning objectives. Participants will be able to:

  • recognise which prompting problem they are facing;
  • choose an appropriate technique;
  • apply it to their own recurring work;
  • improve a prompt without simply starting over.

The whole session resolves into one table on slide 53. If participants remember nothing else, they should remember this mapping:

The problem they haveThe technique
The target is difficult to describe.01 Few-shot & negative examples — show instead of describe.
I keep repeating the same instructions.02 Persistent context — move stable instructions out.
The task is complex or multi-step.03 Decomposition — check the approach before executing.
I need better quality control.04 Self-critique — define criteria and check against them.
The result failed and I don’t know why.05 Debugging — diagnose instead of starting over.

5 Delivery principles

Learn, see, try, improve — in that order, every time

Both decks repeat one cycle: a short input, a live demonstration, individual practice, then a group comparison. The most common way to break a session is to merge see into learn — explaining the demo instead of running it. Run it.

Show the first result, including the bad one

Session 1 slide 7 is called “The output sounds fine — but it guessed the task.” It only works if the group actually sees a fluent, wrong-ish answer. The same is true of the Clinic in both sessions: the first prompt is meant to miss something. If your demo produces a perfect answer on the first run, the lesson disappears.

One message per slide

Every slide carries one statement and, at the bottom, one closing line. If you catch yourself adding a second point, it belongs on the next slide.

Protect the practice time

Practice and the Clinic are where the learning happens; the input slides only set them up. When you are behind, cut input, not practice. Announce each practice with its time and hold it — the badge on the slide shows participants the same number.

Read the room from the polls, do not perform them

The Slido rounds exist to give you information and the group a sense of company. Read the distribution aloud, name what you see in one sentence, move on. No individual answers are shown, and Session 1 slide 4 says so.

Keep everything generic

Every practice and every Clinic submission must be described, not pasted. Say this at the start, and say it again before the Clinic. If a submitted case contains anything identifying, genericise it before you put it on screen.

Never on screen, never in the prompt Personal data, customer data, or confidential information — in the session and in participants’ own work. If you are unsure whether an example is safe, describe the situation instead of showing the source material.

6 Live demo recommendations

Your session is presentation + live demo + Q&A. The demo is not decoration; in Session 1 it carries two of the core messages, and in Session 2 the Clinic is a demo that runs for a quarter of the session.

Rehearse the switch, not just the prompt

The most common failure is mechanical: alt-tabbing into a tool with the wrong window shared, a chat history visible, a font too small to read, a login prompt. Practise the transition from PowerPoint to the tool and back at least three times. Set the tool’s font size before you start. Open a clean session.

Tools

Use M365 Copilot, Helvi, or another approved solution — whichever you know best. Do not use a tool you have not rehearsed with, and do not use one that is not approved for internal use.

Always have a fallback Tools fail, sessions run at capacity, and a live model can be slow. Keep a screenshot of a prepared before/after pair open in a second tab. If the tool stalls, narrate the screenshot and move on — do not spend two minutes waiting in front of 200 people.

7 What good looks like

A session that worked

  • Participants leave with something they wrote themselves — a prompt they saved, not a prompt you gave them.
  • The reflection answers are specific: “naming the audience”, not “it was better”.
  • The Clinic case came from the group, and the group called out the change before you did.
  • The practice rounds ran their full time and were not cut.
  • Someone can restate the core message in their own words at the end.
  • Q&A questions are about applying it, not about what AI is.

Warning signs during delivery

  • You are still on the input slides when the first practice was due.
  • The demo produced a perfect answer on the first run, so there was nothing to improve.
  • The chat is asking what a token is — you have drifted into fundamentals.
  • The Clinic became you writing a prompt while the group watched silently.
  • You answered a data-handling question with a guess.
  • You ran out of time and dropped the transfer practice.
Policy and data questions If a question is about data handling or what a tool is permitted to do and you are not certain of the answer, park it and follow up in writing. No guesses from the stage. Session 1 slide 29 states this to participants, so you are covered by the deck when you use it.

8 What we expect from you

Before the session

  • Work through the Preparation Checklist — it covers presentation review, demo preparation, session planning and the rehearsal.
  • Review your language version. Check content, translation, layout, text overflow, screenshots and examples. Translations have been prepared, but only you can judge whether a term is right for your audience.
  • Fill in your own examples where the deck marks them (Session 2, slides 8, 10, 28, 36).
  • Rehearse once end to end, with the tool, out loud, with a timer.

What you may change — and what you may not

Welcome

  • Language improvements
  • Factual corrections
  • Swapping in examples from your own area
  • Small design improvements

Please do not

  • Rebuild the deck
  • Create your own version
  • Change the didactic sequence
  • Drop the practice rounds to fit more content

Participants may attend more than one session, and the sessions run in several languages. They should feel like one programme.

Work in the existing OneDrive document Do not create your own copy. Make your changes directly in the existing file. This keeps version history intact, avoids conflicts between language versions, and lets us publish the final materials quickly after the sessions. A private copy means your improvements are lost and the published version is wrong.
